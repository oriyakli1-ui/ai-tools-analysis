# Audience: The Second Look

Evidence status: built from public articles and news pages found by web search (links in `MARKET_VALIDATION.md`). I could not open YouTube, Reddit or search-volume tools, so anything marked **[hypothesis]** has no source yet and must be validated.

## Core transformation
From "I got a scary text and I'm not sure what to do" to "I know the five signs and the one safe move, and I can show my parents."

## Segment 1 (primary, the follower): the worried helper, 28 to 55 **[hypothesis]**
- **Who:** adults who already use YouTube, have parents or older relatives, and have received or seen fake texts. They share useful things in family chats.
- **Jobs:**
  - functional: tell if a text is fake in under 10 seconds; know what to do if they already tapped.
  - emotional: stop feeling anxious or foolish about messages.
  - social: be the person who protects the family; have a clear video to send instead of lecturing.
- **Pains:** texts that look real and urgent; parents who don't listen to long warnings; fear of being the one who falls for it.
- **Desires:** one short, calm, shareable video; a rule simple enough to remember.
- **Beliefs to meet:** "Only careless people fall for this." Meet it gently: careful people do too (the film says so).
- **Objections:** "I already know this", "another scare video", "boring bank-style training". Answer with short, warm, well-designed lessons and no panic.
- **Where:** YouTube (search and Shorts), family group chats, Facebook, Reddit.

## Segment 2: the target of the scam, adults 55+ **[hypothesis]**
- **Fact from sources:** a University of Michigan finding, cited by a scam-prevention group, says most seniors rely on TV, newspapers and mail for scam alerts, not YouTube. So this group mostly arrives **through Segment 1** (a shared link or a screen shown by a family member). Design for being watched together, large captions, no jargon.

## Segment 3: young adults 18 to 30 **[hypothesis]**
- Heavy Shorts users, targeted by parcel, job and bank-impersonation texts. Short, fast, funny-calm Shorts work best. Lower trust in "official" advice, so avoid a lecturing tone.

## Language bank
Verbatim phrases found in the sources (headlines and article language). Use them in titles, hooks and descriptions.
- "Is that bank or government text real?"
- "frightening text claims your bank account is locked for fraud"
- "unusual activity" (the standard bait phrase in the messages themselves)
- "Never respond to this text message from your bank"
- "fake bank text messages", "bank impersonation scams", "account locked text scam"
- "Don't click the link", "call the number on your card" (official guidance wording)

Phrases to **validate** on YouTube and Reddit before using as titles **[hypothesis]**: "is this text from my bank real", "what to do if I clicked a scam link", "how to tell if a text is a scam", "my parents keep falling for scams".

## What changes how we write
1. Lead with the viewer's situation ("You got a text that says your account is locked"), not with the scammer.
2. One rule per video. End with a safe action, never a scare.
3. Make it shareable: a clear last line ("Send this to someone who needs it") and a 30-second cut for each lesson.
4. Never teach the scammer's method in detail.
